Utilities are one of the more mechanical parts of moving, but the Houston area has a wrinkle that surprises people moving from most other states: in much of the region, you choose who sells you electricity. Water, sewer and trash work differently, and depend on the specific address rather than a single regional answer. Here is how the pieces fit together.

## Retail electric choice in most of the Houston area

Texas restructured its electric market so that, in areas that participate, customers choose their own retail electric provider (REP) rather than being assigned one automatically. Power to Choose, run by the Public Utility Commission of Texas (PUC), is the official site where certified providers post their residential plans, and it is built specifically so customers in competitive areas can shop. Most of the Greater Houston area is inside this competitive market, meaning a move within the region typically comes with an actual choice of electric company rather than a single default provider.

## Two different companies: the retailer and the delivery utility

The choice you make is only about who sells you the electricity, not who delivers it. A separate company, called a transmission and distribution utility (TDU), owns and maintains the physical poles and wires, reads meters, and responds to outages, regardless of which retail provider you picked. The PUC describes this split directly: the local wires company keeps doing the physical work of delivery no matter which retailer is selling you power. In the Houston area, that delivery utility is CenterPoint Energy Houston Electric, LLC, which is named as the region's regulated transmission and distribution utility in PUC filings. When you have an outage or a downed line, you are dealing with CenterPoint's side of the system, not your retail provider's — even though your bill may come from the retailer.

## Areas outside the choice market

Not every part of Texas participates in retail choice. Municipally owned utilities and electric cooperatives were not required to deregulate when the state opened the market, and the PUC's own guidance confirms that whether a municipal utility or cooperative offers customer choice is voluntary, decided by that utility's own governing board or board of directors — a municipal utility or co-op can choose to opt in, but is not required to. In practical terms, this means some communities in the broader Houston region, particularly smaller cities with their own municipal electric utility or areas served by an electric cooperative, may not be part of the competitive choice market that Power to Choose covers. Confirm which category applies to a specific address rather than assuming; if a municipal utility or cooperative serves the address, ask that utility directly how service and billing work, since Power to Choose will not apply there.

## How to compare plans: the Electricity Facts Label

Every residential plan offered by a retail electric provider must come with an Electricity Facts Label (EFL), a standardized disclosure the PUC requires so customers can make an apples-to-apples comparison across offers. The EFL states whether a plan is fixed-rate or variable, the contract term, applicable fees, and the percentage of renewable energy in that plan compared with the statewide average generation mix. Because plans differ in structure — fixed, variable, or indexed to a published price benchmark — the EFL, not the advertised headline rate alone, is the document to actually read before signing up. Power to Choose links to the EFL for every plan listed.

## Water, sewer and trash: it depends on who serves the address

Unlike electricity, water, sewer and trash service in the Houston area are not a matter of customer choice — they come from whichever provider actually serves that address, and that provider varies block to block. Inside Houston city limits, Houston Public Works bills for water and wastewater service, and the city's Solid Waste Management Department handles trash and recycling collection for properties it serves. Many newer subdivisions outside city limits, however, sit inside a municipal utility district (MUD), which is TCEQ-regulated and typically provides water distribution and sewer service within its own boundaries under its own rules — a structurally different arrangement from city utility billing, and often with its own separate charges. Trash and recycling arrangements likewise vary by provider and are worth confirming directly for a specific address rather than assumed from a neighboring city or subdivision.

## What this means when you're choosing a place

None of this changes based on preference — it is determined entirely by the specific address. Before you move:

- Confirm whether the address is in the competitive retail electric market or served by a municipal utility or cooperative, and shop Power to Choose only if the former applies.
- Identify the transmission and distribution utility for the address so you know who to call for an outage regardless of which retailer you choose.
- Read the Electricity Facts Label for any plan you're considering, not just the advertised rate.
- Ask directly whether water and sewer service comes from the city or from a MUD or other district, since billing, rates and contact information differ.
- Confirm who provides trash and recycling collection at that specific address and how to start or transfer service.

Provider names, market participation and specific plan terms all change over time and by address. Verify current details directly with the PUC, Power to Choose, the specific utility or district, and your agent before you rely on them.
