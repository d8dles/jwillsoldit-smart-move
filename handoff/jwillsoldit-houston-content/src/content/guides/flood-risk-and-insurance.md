Flood risk is one of the more researchable parts of buying or renting in the Houston area, because the underlying data is public. It is also one of the more misunderstood parts, because a flood zone label answers a narrower question than most people assume. This guide covers what the maps mean, what they do not mean, and how insurance and disclosure fit together. None of it substitutes for pulling the actual flood determination on a specific address.

## What FEMA flood zones mean

FEMA flood maps divide land into zones based on modeled flood risk. A Special Flood Hazard Area (SFHA) is the area expected to be inundated by a flood with a 1-percent chance of occurring in any given year — commonly called the base flood or the 100-year flood. Zones beginning with A or V (such as AE, AO, AH or VE) are SFHAs and carry the highest designated risk. Zone B or shaded Zone X covers moderate-risk areas, between the base flood boundary and the 0.2-percent-annual-chance (500-year) flood line. Unshaded Zone X or Zone C denotes minimal mapped risk, and Zone D marks areas where no flood hazard analysis has been done, meaning the risk is simply undetermined rather than absent.

A 1-percent annual chance is not a once-a-century guarantee. It means roughly a 1-in-4 chance of at least one such flood over a typical 30-year mortgage. It is a statistical statement about a specific location, not a promise about timing.

## Maps change, and flooding is not limited to mapped zones

Flood maps get revised as development, drainage changes, new data and construction of flood-control infrastructure like levees and detention basins change how water moves across an area. A map that is accurate today can be updated later, in either direction, for the same parcel.

Just as important: mapped zones describe modeled risk, not a guarantee of safety outside them. FloodSmart, the National Flood Insurance Program's consumer site, reports that a substantial share of flood insurance claims come from outside the mapped high-risk areas — citing that close to a third of NFIP claims from 2020 through 2024 came from locations outside current high-risk flood zones. Moderate- and minimal-risk zones still flood; they are simply modeled as less likely to.

## Flood insurance is a separate policy

Standard homeowners insurance typically excludes flood damage. Flood coverage is bought as its own policy, through two overlapping markets: the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP), managed by FEMA and sold through participating insurance companies or directly through the NFIP, and private flood insurance, sold by insurers outside the NFIP system. The Texas Department of Insurance advises homeowners to ask their agent about both options.

A standard NFIP policy separates building coverage from contents coverage, and each has its own coverage limit — the specific dollar limits are set by federal program rules and change periodically, so confirm current limits directly with FEMA, the NFIP, or your insurer rather than assuming a figure. The core point for a mortgage: a homeowners policy alone will not pay for flood damage, and it will not satisfy a lender's flood-insurance requirement where one applies.

## The Harris County Flood Control District's role

Much of the Houston area's flood-control infrastructure is managed by the Harris County Flood Control District, a special-purpose district created by the Texas Legislature in 1937 and governed by Harris County Commissioners Court. Its stated mission is flood damage reduction, and it carries this out by planning, building and maintaining infrastructure across more than 1,500 mapped bayous and creeks totaling roughly 2,500 miles. Its responsibilities include long-running projects on individual waterways as well as major flood-control infrastructure such as the Addicks and Barker reservoirs, built in the 1940s. The District's jurisdiction is Harris County; a property outside Harris County falls under different county or municipal flood-control authorities, which should be checked separately.

## Disclosure and lender requirements

The standard Texas Seller's Disclosure Notice used in most residential sales (TREC form OP-H) asks specific flood-related questions, including whether the seller is aware of previous flooding caused by a failure, breach, or controlled or emergency release of water from a reservoir, and whether the property has had previous water penetration from a natural flood event. These are seller-reported answers, not an independent inspection, so treat them as a starting point rather than a full risk assessment. A related, separate disclosure requirement applies to certain residential leases, so renters should ask about flood history too rather than assuming disclosure rules only apply to sales.

On lending: in communities that participate in the NFIP, a federally backed or government-backed mortgage generally requires flood insurance for a structure located in a designated high-risk (SFHA) zone. Outside those zones, federal law does not mandate the coverage, though a specific lender may still require or recommend it, and a homeowner may reasonably choose it anyway given how often flooding occurs outside mapped high-risk areas. Confirm requirements with your specific lender, since practices can be more conservative than the federal floor.

## What this means when you're choosing a place

Flood risk has to be checked property by property. A neighborhood-level impression is not a substitute for a parcel-level answer. Before committing to an address:

- Pull the FEMA flood zone determination for the exact parcel, not just the general area.
- Ask directly whether the community participates in the NFIP, and whether flood insurance is available and at what coverage level for that address.
- Read the seller's disclosure flood answers carefully, and ask the seller or agent follow-up questions about any flooding history, even outside the specific disclosure questions asked.
- Ask whether the property is near a reservoir, spillway, bayou, or other flood-control infrastructure, and if so, whether the Harris County Flood Control District (or the relevant county authority) has any related project history there.
- Get a flood insurance quote before you are under contract, not after, so cost and availability are not a surprise.

Flood zones, program rules and insurance costs all change, and none of this is insurance or legal advice. Verify current flood-zone status, disclosure answers and coverage requirements with FEMA, the NFIP, a licensed insurance agent, and your real estate agent before you rely on them.
