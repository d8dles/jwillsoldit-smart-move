Texas has no state income tax, and local governments lean on property tax instead. If you are moving here from somewhere with a different mix, the system takes some translating. The mechanics below are the part that stays stable; the specific numbers change, so treat every figure as something to confirm before you rely on it.

## Two separate jobs: valuing and taxing

Texas property tax runs on a split system. A county appraisal district decides what each property is worth. Separate local taxing units — the county, the school district, a city if the address is inside one, and any special districts — each set their own rate and apply it to that value. The appraisal district does not levy taxes, and the taxing units do not set values. The revenue stays local and pays for schools, streets and roads, police and fire protection, and other services.

In Harris County, values are set by the Harris Central Appraisal District (HCAD). Surrounding counties have their own appraisal districts, and the Texas Comptroller maintains a directory of all of them. State law requires each district to reappraise property in its jurisdiction at least once every three years.

## The homestead exemption

An exemption removes part of a home's value from taxation before a unit's rate is applied. The main one is the residence homestead exemption, for a home you own and occupy as your principal residence. As of this writing, the Texas Comptroller states that school districts must exempt $140,000 of a residence homestead's value, with an additional $60,000 exemption for owners who are age 65 or older or disabled. Any taxing unit may also adopt a local-option homestead exemption of up to 20 percent of a property's appraised value.

Exemption amounts are set by the Legislature and have changed more than once in recent years, so confirm the current figures at comptroller.texas.gov and with your county appraisal district. You apply for exemptions through the appraisal district, which administers them.

## The appraisal cap

A separate rule limits how fast a homestead's taxable value can climb. Once an owner has qualified for the homestead exemption in both the preceding and current year, the appraised value used for taxation generally cannot rise more than 10 percent per year, plus the market value of any new improvements.

The cap follows the owner's qualification, not the house. A long-time owner's capped value can sit well below market value, and that history does not transfer with the deed. When ownership changes, the property is generally appraised without the prior owner's cap, so the seller's current tax bill is a weak predictor of what a new owner will pay. Budget from the appraisal district's full appraised value, not from the bill the last owner was paying.

## You can protest the value

When the appraisal district sets a new value, it sends the owner a notice of appraised value, and owners who disagree can file a protest. The deadline for most protests is May 15 or the 30th day after the notice was delivered, whichever is later. Protests are heard by an appraisal review board — a panel of local citizens, separate from the appraisal district's staff — and owners can appear in person, by telephone or videoconference, or by written affidavit. The Comptroller publishes plain-language guides to the process, and each appraisal district posts its own instructions. The protest system is a normal part of how the process works here, not a special measure.

## Why totals differ so much by address

Rates stack. A single address is typically taxed by several overlapping units at once: the county, a school district, possibly a city, and possibly special districts such as hospital, junior college or water districts. Each unit sets its own rate, so the combined rate depends entirely on which units cover that address.

This is where the Houston area gets specific. School district rates differ from one district to the next. Some addresses sit inside a city and pay a city rate; others are unincorporated and pay no city tax, but many newer unincorporated subdivisions sit inside a municipal utility district (MUD) that levies its own tax to repay water, sewer and drainage debt. Two similar houses a short distance apart can carry meaningfully different combined rates because they sit under different stacks of taxing units. The appraisal district's record for a parcel lists the units that tax it — in Harris County, HCAD's property search shows a jurisdictions table for each account.

## Escrow, briefly

Most buyers pay the tax bill through escrow. The lender typically collects an estimated portion of the year's taxes with each monthly mortgage payment, holds it, and pays the taxing units when bills come due. When appraised values or tax rates change, the escrow portion changes with them — which is why a monthly payment can move even on a fixed-rate loan. For new construction, ask the lender whether the first-year estimate reflects the finished home or something less, and when the escrow account will be recalculated. Renters do not pay the bill directly; it belongs to the landlord.

## What this means when you're choosing a place

The listing tells you the asking price; the tax stack tells you part of what the address costs to hold, year after year. Before committing to a specific address:

- Pull the parcel on the county appraisal district's website and read the list of taxing units on the account.
- Ask for the current rate of each unit, and specifically whether any special districts — MUDs among them — tax the address.
- Treat the current owner's tax bill as history, not a forecast. It may be built on exemptions and a capped value that will not apply to you.
- If you will have a mortgage, ask the lender how the escrow estimate was built and when it adjusts.
- After you close on a home you will live in, ask the appraisal district how to apply for the homestead exemption and any others you may qualify for.

Rates, exemption amounts and deadlines all change. Verify current specifics with the county appraisal district, the taxing units themselves and comptroller.texas.gov, and walk the numbers through with your lender and your agent rather than relying on any general guide, this one included.
