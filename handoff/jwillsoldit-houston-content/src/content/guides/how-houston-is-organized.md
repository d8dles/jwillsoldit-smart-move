Newcomers usually meet Houston as a wall of freeway names, county lines and shorthand that locals never stop to explain. The fastest way to make sense of it is to picture the region the way traffic engineers built it: three concentric rings crossed by freeway spokes radiating from Downtown. Once you can place an address by its nearest ring and nearest spoke, most conversations about location start making sense.

## Three rings, moving outward

The innermost ring is Interstate 610, which everyone calls the Loop or Loop 610. Even the Texas Department of Transportation labels its segments the way locals talk about them — the West Loop, the South Loop and so on.

The middle ring is the Sam Houston Tollway, the tolled main lanes operated by the Harris County Toll Road Authority (HCTRA). The free frontage roads running alongside are commonly known as Beltway 8, and people use the two names loosely for the same corridor. If someone says a place is "outside the Beltway," this is the ring they mean.

The outermost ring is the Grand Parkway, State Highway 99. TxDOT describes it as a circumferential highway planned to run roughly 180 miles through seven counties, built and opened in lettered segments — some carry traffic today and others are still in development. It passes through many of the newer master-planned communities on the region's edges.

## The spokes and their local names

Freeways here carry two names: the route number on the map and the local name on traffic reports. Both are worth learning.

- **I-10 west of Downtown is the Katy Freeway**, running toward the city of Katy and the region's western edge.
- **I-10 east of Downtown** continues toward Chambers County; TxDOT refers to this stretch as I-10 East.
- **I-45 north of Downtown is the North Freeway**, the corridor TxDOT is rebuilding under its North Houston Highway Improvement Project.
- **I-45 south of Downtown is the Gulf Freeway**, heading toward the coast.
- **I-69/US 59 is the Southwest Freeway** on the southwest side and **the Eastex Freeway** on the northeast side — same route, two names, depending on which side of Downtown you are on.
- **US 290 is the Northwest Freeway**, running toward Cypress.
- **SH 288 runs south from the Downtown area** toward Brazoria County, with express toll lanes in its center and connections serving the Texas Medical Center area.

When a listing or a coworker says "off 290" or "on the Gulf Freeway side," they are handing you a compass bearing. The spokes are how Houstonians describe direction.

## One region, several counties

Greater Houston is not one city or one county. The Houston-Galveston Area Council, the regional planning body, defines an eight-county metropolitan planning area: Harris, Fort Bend, Montgomery, Brazoria, Galveston, Liberty, Chambers and Waller counties. The city of Houston itself sits in Harris County, and growth has pushed daily life well across the county lines around it.

County lines matter more here than newcomers expect. Which county an address sits in determines its appraisal district, its tax bills, its emergency management office and often which utility and school arrangements apply. Two houses a short distance apart can sit in different counties with different answers to all of those questions, so confirm the county for any address you consider.

## What "inside the Loop" means

"Inside the Loop" is local shorthand for the area enclosed by Loop 610 — the oldest, most urban part of the city, containing Downtown and many of Houston's established neighborhoods. Living "inside the Loop" generally means shorter distances to the central employment districts, older housing stock mixed with newer infill, and a more street-grid feel. Living outside it generally means newer subdivisions and more distance, though the region is far too varied for that to be a rule. The phrase is baked into how institutions talk: METRO, the regional transit agency, describes its Park & Ride commuter service as designed for people who live outside the 610 Loop and work in the core.

## Two airports, two sides of town

The region has two commercial airports, both operated by Houston Airports, the City of Houston's aviation department. George Bush Intercontinental Airport (IAH) sits about 22 miles north of Downtown. William P. Hobby Airport (HOU) sits about 11 miles southeast of Downtown. Frequent flyers learn quickly that which airport is "close" depends entirely on which side of the region they live on, and that travel time to either varies widely with time of day and direction.

## Why rings and spokes help

Every location decision in Houston eventually reduces to the same two questions: which ring, and which spoke. A job in the Energy Corridor, a house near the Grand Parkway and family near Hobby Airport describe three different lives depending on how the rings and spokes connect them. The area pages on this site group Greater Houston into nine regions, and those regions largely follow this same logic — each one is defined by the rings and spokes that serve it.

## What this means when you're choosing a place

Before you fall for any specific house or apartment, place your daily life on this map. Mark where you will work, where the people you visit most often live, and which airport you would use, then note the nearest ring and spoke for each. Addresses that sound far apart can share a spoke and connect easily; addresses that sound close can sit on opposite sides of a ring with no direct route between them. Ask which freeway a commute would actually use, whether it crosses one ring or two, and what the surface-street fallback looks like. Then read this site's traffic and commute guide, because in Houston the map only tells you half the story — the clock tells the rest, and the only reliable way to learn it is to drive the route at the hours you would really travel it.
