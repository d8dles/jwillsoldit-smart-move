Houston's climate is not a footnote to living here; it is a housing consideration on par with commute and price. The National Weather Service places Houston in a humid subtropical setting, with the Gulf supplying moisture year-round. In practice that means long, hot, humid summers, a short and generally mild winter, heavy rain that can arrive in bulk, and a hurricane season that is simply part of the calendar. None of this should scare a newcomer off — millions of people live here comfortably — but all of it should shape what you look for in a house and what you ask before you commit to one.

## The climate, plainly

Summer is the dominant season: hot, humid and long, with air conditioning treated as essential infrastructure rather than a luxury. Winters are mild by the standards of most of the country, but not weightless. Hard freezes reach the region occasionally, and when they do the consequences are outsized because the housing stock is built for heat: the February 2021 winter storm brought days of ice and record cold to Southeast Texas, with multiday road closures, power outages, loss of heat and broken pipes across the region. Rainfall deserves equal respect. NOAA's precipitation studies note the region's exposure to extreme rainfall, especially from tropical systems, so how a property handles water matters even far from any coast or bayou.

## Hurricane season is June through November

The Atlantic hurricane season officially runs from June 1 to November 30, with the bulk of activity historically falling between mid-August and mid-October — and NOAA notes that storms occasionally form outside those dates. For Houston-area residents this is a season to be organized for, not a reason for year-round worry. The sensible rhythm, urged every year by Ready Harris and other local emergency management offices, is to prepare in late spring: check supplies, review plans, and know what you would do before anything has a name.

## Know your evacuation zone

Evacuation on the upper Texas coast is planned, not improvised. The state's Texas Hurricane Center points residents to designated hurricane evacuation zones and routes, and coastal counties in this region — including Harris, Galveston, Brazoria and Chambers — use mapped zip-code-based zones so officials can call evacuations in stages rather than all at once. Where your address falls in that system shapes the guidance you would receive in a storm: communities closest to the coast are the focus of evacuation orders, while for many inland addresses the official emphasis is on preparing to ride out wind, rain and power loss — Ready.gov notes that hurricanes can affect areas more than 100 miles inland. So make this concrete: when you are considering an address, look up its evacuation zone through the state and county emergency management sites, and if you move there, learn your routes before the season starts. If an evacuation order ever covers your zone, the guidance is unambiguous: go.

## Prepare like a local

Preparedness here is ordinary, not dramatic. Ready.gov's baseline kit is a reasonable floor: water (a gallon per person per day for several days), several days of non-perishable food, medications, and a battery-powered or hand-crank radio, plus a household plan for where you would meet and how you would communicate. Local emergency management — Ready Harris in Harris County, and the equivalent office in each surrounding county — publishes hurricane guides and alert sign-ups. Signing up for county alerts is a one-time task that pays for itself in every storm, freeze and flood event afterward.

## Windstorm insurance is its own question near the coast

Insurance works differently close to the Gulf, and buyers should know this before falling for a house on the region's coastal edge. The Texas Department of Insurance designates catastrophe areas along the coast — fourteen first-tier coastal counties, which in this region include Galveston, Brazoria, Chambers and Matagorda, plus portions of Harris County east of State Highway 146, including La Porte, Morgan's Point, Pasadena, Seabrook and Shoreacres. In those designated areas, the Texas Windstorm Insurance Association (TWIA) operates as the insurer of last resort for wind and hail coverage, and TDI notes that new construction, additions and re-roofs there generally must meet adopted building specifications and windstorm inspection requirements to qualify. The practical translation: for any address in or near these areas, ask early how wind and hail would be covered, what documentation the structure has, and what the full insurance picture costs — and verify the current rules with TDI and your insurer, because territory and requirements are theirs to define, not this guide's.

## What heat and humidity mean for a house

The climate stresses houses in specific, inspectable ways, so turn it into questions during showings and inspections:

- **Cooling.** How old is the air-conditioning system, has it been maintained, and is it sized for the house? Are ducts, attic insulation and windows up to the cooling load?
- **Moisture.** How does the house manage humidity — ventilation in bathrooms and kitchen, condition of seals and flashing, any history or signs of persistent moisture in walls, attics or crawl spaces?
- **Water on the lot.** Where does rain go? Ask about grading, gutters and downspouts, and look at the lot and street during or just after heavy rain if you can.
- **Freeze exposure.** Where do water lines run, and are exposed pipes protected for the occasional hard freeze?
- **Power resilience.** What is the plan for outages — and if there is a generator or you would add one, how is it set up?

An inspector can speak to all of these; your job is to make sure they are asked.

## What this means when you're choosing a place

Climate is a filter to apply before you commit, not a surprise to absorb afterward. For any address on your shortlist: look up its evacuation zone, ask how the specific house handles cooling, moisture and drainage, and — anywhere near the coastal counties or the eastern edge of Harris County — get a real answer on windstorm coverage before you sign. Then do the ordinary local things once you arrive: build the kit, sign up for county alerts, and mark the start of hurricane season as a household calendar item. People who live well here are not the ones who found a place the weather ignores; they are the ones who chose a house, an insurance setup and a plan that already assume the weather. Verify the specifics with your inspector, your insurer and your county's emergency management office.
