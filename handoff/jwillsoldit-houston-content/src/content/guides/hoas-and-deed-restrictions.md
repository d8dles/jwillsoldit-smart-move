Houston is widely known for having no citywide zoning ordinance. What that means in practice is that private deed restrictions carry more of the load than they do in most American cities. If you are used to zoning telling you what can be built or operated near your home, Houston asks you to look at a different document instead: the recorded restrictions on the specific subdivision.

## Why deed restrictions matter unusually much here

Deed restrictions, also called restrictive covenants, are private agreements recorded against the lots in a subdivision, typically put in place by the original developer and running with the land to future owners. In a city with zoning, land use is primarily a public regulatory question. In Houston, without that citywide framework, whether a subdivision stays entirely residential, whether a business can operate from a home, minimum lot sizes, setbacks and similar questions are frequently governed instead by these private, subdivision-specific restrictions. Two lots a few blocks apart can carry meaningfully different rules, because each subdivision's restrictions were written and, in some cases, amended separately.

## Who enforces them, including the City's role

Deed restrictions are typically enforced by a property owners' association, by individual property owners with standing under the recorded declaration, or by both. The City of Houston is also directly involved in a way that surprises people coming from zoned cities: because Houston is not zoned, the state Legislature and City Council have specifically authorized the City to help enforce recorded deed restrictions, for neighborhood protection, through its Legal Department. Any resident — an individual, a neighborhood group or a civic club — can file a complaint with the City's Deed Restriction Enforcement Team. Most violations are resolved after a warning letter; the City can pursue litigation if a violation continues. There is a notable limit on this authority: if a property has been in continuous violation of its deed restrictions since before August 1965, the City cannot sue to stop it, though it can pursue violations that began after that date. This means the City's enforcement role is real but not unlimited, and it does not replace the association's or a neighbor's own enforcement rights under the restrictions themselves.

## The Texas Property Code framework

Property owners' associations in Texas operate under several chapters of the Property Code. Chapter 209, the Texas Residential Property Owners Protection Act, sets baseline requirements for many residential subdivisions' associations — for example, provisions that would disqualify an owner from voting are void, and an association generally must give written notice by certified mail before it suspends an owner's use of common areas, files suit, levies certain fines, or reports a delinquency to a credit reporting service. Separate chapters — 204 and 202 among them — address the association's power to amend and enforce restrictive covenants in certain subdivisions, and how those covenants are construed. These statutes set a statewide floor; the specific rights and obligations for any given property still come from that subdivision's own recorded declaration and the association's bylaws, which can be more detailed than the statute requires.

## Assessments, resale certificates and fees to budget for

Where an active property owners' association exists, membership is usually mandatory for owners in the subdivision, and the association can levy regular assessments, plus special assessments when the declaration allows it. Before closing, a buyer typically wants a resale certificate — a document from the association confirming the regular assessment amount, any unpaid or upcoming special assessments, and a copy of the current restrictions and bylaws. Texas Property Code Chapter 207 requires an association to deliver these materials within a set number of business days of a proper written request, using a resale certificate prepared shortly beforehand. Associations are permitted to charge a reasonable fee for preparing, copying or recording these documents, though the law does not fix a specific dollar amount — so ask the association or its management company for the current fee in writing rather than assuming a figure. Some subdivisions also charge a separate transfer fee when ownership changes; whether one applies, and how much it is, varies by association, so confirm it directly rather than guessing.

## Architectural control

Many subdivisions' restrictions include an architectural control or review provision, requiring an owner to submit plans for exterior changes — additions, fences, some landscaping, and similar work — to the developer or to an architectural control committee before starting. Texas statute limits how far these committees can go: an association generally cannot demand more documentation for one type of exterior application than it would for another, and cannot withhold approval from an installation that meets the declaration's own stated requirements except under defined circumstances. Whether a given subdivision has an active architectural control process at all, and how strict it is, still depends entirely on that subdivision's specific restrictions.

## What this means when you're choosing a place

The recorded restrictions and the association's actual practice matter more here than in a zoned city, and they are specific to each subdivision rather than something you can infer from the general area. Before you're under contract:

- Request a full copy of the recorded restrictive covenants for the specific subdivision, not just a summary.
- Ask whether an active property owners' association exists, and if so, request a current resale certificate showing dues, any pending special assessments, and current bylaws.
- Ask about any transfer or resale-processing fee charged at closing, and get the amount in writing.
- Ask whether an architectural control committee reviews exterior changes, and what its process requires.
- If the City of Houston's enforcement role is relevant to a concern you have, contact the City's Deed Restriction Enforcement Team directly rather than assuming what is or is not covered.

Deed restrictions, association rules and fees vary by subdivision and can be amended over time. Verify the current restrictions, bylaws and fees with the property owners' association, a title company, or your agent, and treat this guide as background rather than a substitute for reading the actual recorded documents.
